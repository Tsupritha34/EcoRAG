Biodiversity Knowledge

Species Richness:
Species richness is the number of different species present in an ecosystem. Higher species richness can indicate greater biological diversity, although richness alone does not describe how evenly individuals are distributed among species.

Habitat Diversity:
Habitat diversity refers to the variety of habitats available within a landscape. Greater habitat diversity can provide resources and shelter for a wider range of organisms.

Pollinators:
Pollinators such as bees, butterflies, and other insects depend on flowering plants, nesting sites, and suitable habitat. Diverse vegetation and reduced pesticide exposure can support pollinator populations.

Native Vegetation:
Native trees, shrubs, grasses, and flowering plants can provide food, shelter, nesting sites, and movement corridors for local wildlife.

Habitat Corridors:
Vegetated corridors can connect fragmented habitats and allow organisms to move between habitat patches. Corridors can help reduce the effects of habitat fragmentation.

Agroforestry:
Agroforestry integrates trees with crops or livestock. Trees can provide habitat, improve landscape connectivity, reduce erosion, increase organic matter inputs, and create additional resources for biodiversity.

Vegetation Diversity:
Increasing plant diversity can provide different food sources, shelter, and microhabitats. Diverse vegetation can support insects, birds, soil organisms, and other wildlife.

Scientific reasoning:
Biodiversity conservation is often improved by increasing habitat availability, vegetation diversity, connectivity, and native plant resources. The effects depend on local climate, land use, species composition, and management practices.
